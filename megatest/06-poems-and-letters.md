# Salt and Lamplight

*Poems and Letters from the Far Shore*

> The poems and letters that follow were found together in a dented tin box at the Gannet Rock light, after the station was decommissioned and its lamp put out for good. Some pieces are signed; some are not. They are arranged here as they seemed to want to be read — by the turning of a single lamp, and across the long distance any light must travel to reach the person it was lit for.

---

## I. Gannet Rock
*Free verse*

There is a kind of arithmetic to solitude:  
one lamp, one keeper, one rock  
the maps mark with a dot so small  
it might be a flyspeck, a flaw in the paper,  
a place the cartographer paused to think.

I have learned to read the weather  
the way you read a face across a table —  
the sky's small tightening before the squall,  
the way the gulls go quiet, then go inland,  
the barometer's slow surrender.

By day I am a janitor of light:  
I polish brass, I trim the wick,  
I keep the great glass eye unclouded  
so that by night it can perform the only sentence  
a lighthouse knows by heart — to say, again and again,  
*I am here, the rock is here, go around.*

The ships do not write back.  
They take my warning and they take their leave,  
their lamps diminishing like the last sparks  
of a conversation no one wanted to end.

And still I send it. Every night.  
This is the whole discipline of the far shore:  
to give your light to those who pass,  
and ask them only to keep passing —  
to keep on being somewhere else, and alive.

## II. Sonnet for the Turning Light
*Sonnet (Shakespearean)*

When evening folds the harbor into grey,  
I climb the spiral stair to wake the flame;  
the lamp begins its slow, revolving way  
and writes across the dark its only name.

No word, no sentence — just a turning beam  
that finds the ship and lets the ship go by,  
a pulse repeated like a patient dream,  
a syntax with no speaker and no why.

I am the hand that keeps the rhythm true,  
the trimmer of the wick, the oil, the glass;  
I count the seconds as they ferry through  
and let the heavy nights and seasons pass.

So if you seek me when the dark is deep,  
look for the light I turn but cannot keep.

## III. Tides
*A haiku sequence*

dawn on the cold rock —  
the lamp, outshone at last, sleeps  
while the gulls wake up

low tide at midday —  
the sea lays out its findings:  
kelp, a boot, one shoe

fog. the whole world shrinks  
to the length of one foghorn —  
then opens again

a letter arrives  
salt-stained, weeks on the water —  
spring, in her small hand

evening: I relight  
the wick that holds the island  
steady in the black

all night the beam turns —  
somewhere a ship reads my pulse  
and turns its own wheel

## IV. The Tending of the Dark
*Villanelle*

The light comes back to those who tend the dark.  
I trim the wick against the falling night,  
hold steady, love; the morning leaves its mark.

Each evening I become the watchman's spark  
and feed the flame that argues with the white —  
the light comes back to those who tend the dark.

The gulls go silent, and the harbor's arc  
is lost beneath a fog that swallows sight;  
hold steady, love; the morning leaves its mark.

I count the ships the way you'd name a lark,  
by song, by shadow, by their leaning flight —  
the light comes back to those who tend the dark.

And though the winter strips the headland stark  
and salt has gnawed the railings left and right,  
hold steady, love; the morning leaves its mark.

So write to me, and let the words embark  
across the black expanse before it's bright —  
the light comes back to those who tend the dark;  
hold steady, love; the morning leaves its mark.

## V. Things the Tide Brought to Gannet Rock
*List poem*

A list, kept by the keeper, of what the water left:

one fishing float, green glass, the size of a held breath;  
the long pale bone of something larger than a man;  
a child's wooden boat, mast snapped, paint the blue of a forgotten flag;  
forty-one bottle caps, no two of them from the same country;  
a door, complete with its lock, opening now onto nothing;  
the cursive of the kelp, signing and unsigning the sand;  
a typewriter, keys fused, the letter *E* still legible;  
a single boot — always the left, always too late for its partner;  
a hymnal swollen to twice its faith, every page a grey wave;  
the smell of a city I have never been to, and then the smell of rain;  
a letter in a bottle, addressed to no one, which I have not opened;  
and, each grey morning, the whole field of the sea, returned, returned, returned.

## VI. Pantoum of the Inland Garden
*Pantoum*

I plant the seeds the keeper cannot grow,  
salt-bitten air will not allow a rose;  
I keep these gardens for the both of us,  
I press their colors into folded notes.

Salt-bitten air will not allow a rose;  
but here the soil is dark and holds the rain.  
I press their colors into folded notes,  
I send the summer down the coast by train.

But here the soil is dark and holds the rain,  
a hundred miles from where his lamp is turning,  
I send the summer down the coast by train  
to tell him green is one more kind of burning.

A hundred miles from where his lamp is turning,  
I keep these gardens for the both of us;  
to tell him green is one more kind of burning,  
I plant the seeds the keeper cannot grow.

## VII. The Bottle
*Prose poem*

Among the things the sea has handed me, there is one I have never opened. A bottle, green as a held breath, with a curl of paper inside gone soft at the edges like a moth's wing. I found it in November. It is June now. I have set it on the windowsill where the lamp's turning catches it, once each revolution, so that all night it flares and goes dark, flares and goes dark — a small green heartbeat keeping time with the great white one above it.

I tell myself I do not open it because the salt has sealed the cork, because the paper would tear. The truth is plainer. As long as it stays closed, it could say anything. It could be a love letter, a confession, the coordinates of buried treasure, a child's joke, a cry for help twenty years too old to answer. Closed, it is every message at once. I have grief enough that I can do nothing about; I would rather keep this one as a kind of light — unopened, undimmed, still possible. Some mornings I think the whole sea is a letter we keep almost reading. Then the tide goes out, and takes the sentence with it, and leaves the green glass burning on the sill.

## VIII. The First Letter
*Elias Thorn to Wren Calloway — winter*

Gannet Rock Light  
The 9th of January

Dear Miss Calloway,

You will not remember me, though we have met. Three autumns ago you gave a lecture in the Mariner's Hall on the seeds that travel by sea — how a thing can cross an entire ocean sealed in salt and wax and still, against all good sense, *intend* to grow. I was the man at the back who asked whether anything could take root on bare rock. You said, "Given soil enough to fill a thimble, and someone patient enough to guard the thimble — yes." I have thought about that thimble for three years.

I keep the light on Gannet Rock, eleven miles from your harbor and a great deal further than that by every other measure. There is no soil here to speak of — only the lamp, the brass, and the long arithmetic of the nights. But there is a ledge on the eastern wall, sheltered from the worst of the salt, where the rain collects a fistful of grit, and I have begun to believe a thimble's worth of garden might survive there, if it were stubborn enough, and if its keeper were too.

I am writing, then, to ask you for seeds. Whatever you judge might bear the wind. I can offer little in exchange but a faithful correspondent and an excellent view of the weather as it travels east toward you — I will always know your storms a full day before you do, and I will warn you when I can.

The light turns above me as I write. It says the same thing it has always said. I find that I would like, just once, to say something to someone who is able to answer.

Yours from the far shore,  
Elias Thorn, Keeper

## IX. The Second Letter
*Wren Calloway to Elias Thorn — late winter*

Calloway Seed & Provision  
Marrow Lane, by the inland road  
The 2nd of February

Dear Mr. Thorn,

I remember the question, and now I remember the man. You stood at the back with your hat held in both hands, as though the hall itself were a sort of weather you were waiting out. I am glad the thimble lodged somewhere and grew.

I have enclosed three twists of paper. The first is sea-thrift, which you will already know — it asks for nothing, holds half the cliffs of this coast together with its small fists, and flowers a pink that looks, at a distance, like a thought you cannot quite recall. The second is a hardy stonecrop; it stores its own water and will forgive you every week you forget it. The third I will not name. Plant it last, in the most sheltered corner of your thimble, and write to me when it shows. I want to learn whether you will know it for what it is — or whether the rock will make something altogether new of it. Seeds are like letters in that respect: you send them off, and the place where they land has its own ideas.

You offer me your weather, and I accept. It is a fair trade — your storms for my flowers, your warnings for my long waiting. I live a hundred miles inland now, where the soil is dark and deep and holds the rain like a held breath, and where, some nights, I confess I miss the sea so badly that I plant things with no business growing this far from salt, only to have the smell of it back on my hands.

Tend your thimble, Mr. Thorn. I will tend mine. Between the two of us we may yet keep a whole coastline in flower — a hundred miles apart, and never once in the same garden.

Faithfully,  
Wren Calloway

## X. The Third Letter
*Elias Thorn to Wren Calloway — midsummer*

Gannet Rock Light  
The 21st of June, the longest day

Dear Wren — if I may —

It has flowered.

Not the thrift, though that took first and holds the ledge now as if it were born to it; and not the stonecrop, which has already forgiven me four separate droughts. The third one. The one you would not name. This morning I climbed out before the dawn watch was properly over, and there it was in the grey half-light: a single bloom the deep blue of the last inch of evening, the blue that comes just before the lamp takes the sky over. I do not know its name. I have decided not to ask. The rock made something of it, exactly as you said it might, and I would rather call it by the place where it grew than by whatever it used to be.

I have set your three twists of paper, emptied now, into a green bottle on my windowsill — beside another bottle I have told no one about, one the sea brought me in November, sealed, which I have never opened and never will. I keep the two of them side by side. They have come to mean the same thing to me: that the best letters may be the ones still holding all their possibility, the ones not yet read to the end.

So I will not read *us* to the end either. I will keep the light. You will keep the soil. And the flower on the eastern wall will go on being the strange blue answer to a question I asked in a hall three years ago, with my hat in my hands, waiting out the weather of my own life.

Come and see it, if you ever leave the inland road. The thimble is small, but the view from here is very wide.

Yours, and signing off now to trim the wick,  
Elias
