# A Naturalist's Field Guide to the Fauna of Veranthos
### Being a Catalogue of Remarkable Creatures Encountered During Four Expeditions to the Interior Continent
*Compiled by Professor Edelinde Voss, Chair of Comparative Zoology, Halvenmoor Institute of Natural Sciences, Third Revised Edition*

---

## Preface by the Author

The continent of Veranthos had been, for most of recorded history, little more than a rumor stitched onto the edge of navigational charts — a mass of land described by sailors as "the place where compasses argue with themselves." When the Halvenmoor Institute funded my first expedition in the Year of the Copper Meridian, I carried with me four notebooks, two crates of preserving salt, a watercolor set of dubious quality, and the confident arrogance of someone who had read about wilderness without ever having endured it.

What I found dismantled every assumption I had carried aboard the *Stormgrace Merewyn* and scattered the pieces across something vast, strange, and deeply uninterested in my expectations.

Veranthos is a continent roughly the size of a sleeping giant. Its spine is the Orveth Range, a chain of mountains whose peaks produce clouds that never fully dissipate, giving the interior a quality of light I can only describe as *filtered* — as though the sun were being read aloud rather than shone directly. Below those peaks, the land descends through cloud-forest, open savanna, subterranean river systems, and the peculiar biome I have come to call the Crystalline Margins, which is neither desert nor wetland but shares the discomforts of both.

The creatures catalogued here represent only a fraction of what I observed, and a smaller fraction still of what certainly exists beyond my reach. I have tried to write with precision where I achieved it and with honesty where I failed. The field notes appended to each entry are drawn verbatim from my expedition journals, imperfect grammar intact.

I dedicate this edition to my field assistant Tomás Quellinvane, without whose knowledge of Veranthi trail-signs I would have been eaten on at least three separate occasions by creatures that did not make this catalogue because they were never slow enough to observe.

*— E. Voss, Halvenmoor, late autumn*

## I. The Mirewalker Crane *(Gruinis vastandus)*

**Habitat:** The Sallow Fens, a vast waterlogged depression in the continent's southwestern quadrant, notable for its amber-colored water and forests of drowned ironwood.

**Appearance:** At first sighting, the Mirewalker Crane appears to be a length of dead birch fallen against a larger tree — a pale, vertical shaft approximately two meters tall, feathered in white and ash-grey plumage so finely barred that the bird seems to be wearing a garment made of paper. The neck alone accounts for nearly half this height, capable of folding into a compressed S-shape that makes the bird appear dramatically shorter when alarmed. The legs are long, jointed in three places (the lowest joint being unique among Veranthi avians), and end in spreading toes laced with a thin membrane of skin the color of tarnished copper. The bill is straight, narrow, and roughly as long as my forearm.

**Behavior:** Mirewalker Cranes are among the most patient hunters on the continent. An individual may stand motionless in shallow water for upward of six hours, body inclined at a precise angle that appears, from any distance greater than twenty meters, to be a trick of the light rather than an animal. When prey comes within range, the strike is not the sudden lunge one associates with herons; instead the bird lowers itself with an almost ceremonial slowness and seizes its target from beneath, pulling upward. Pairs mate for life and construct nests on elevated ironwood stumps, the nests being enormous flat platforms reinforced with river clay. Breeding pairs produce a haunting bilateral call — each bird singing a different note that harmonizes with the other — audible for several kilometers across open water.

**Diet:** Primarily shallow-water fish of the genus *Plathinos*, supplemented by large mollusks, which the crane cracks by dropping from height onto exposed root systems with apparently deliberate aim.

*Field Note, Second Expedition, Day 47: Watched a mated pair fish the same channel from opposite banks for nearly four hours. Neither bird moved more than half a meter from its chosen position. At no point did they appear to acknowledge each other. Yet when a juvenile intruded on the male's fishing lane, the female — on the opposite bank, apparently inattentive — gave a single sharp call and the juvenile retreated. I cannot explain the communication. I have given up trying.*

## II. The Threadbear *(Arachnursus fibrosus)*

**Habitat:** The Understory of the Orveth cloud-forests, particularly in stands of the tall-rooted Valkhwood tree, whose exposed root systems create a labyrinth of sheltered cavities.

**Appearance:** The Threadbear is a small mammal, no larger than a domestic cat, with a rounded body covered in fur that is not, precisely, fur. Each individual filament of the coat is hollow and semi-transparent, terminating in a tiny hook visible only under magnification. The overall effect is of an animal covered in fine glass wool, giving it a faintly luminous quality in low light. The face is blunt and wide, with eyes positioned almost entirely on the sides of the head — an arrangement suggesting prey-animal heritage contradicted by every behavioral observation. The paws are five-fingered with retractable claws and opposable thumbs on the rear feet only.

**Behavior:** The Threadbear is solitary and territorial but communicates with extraordinary sophistication through the manipulation of its coat. The hollow filaments can be vibrated individually to produce ultrasonic pulses, and neighboring individuals conduct what appear to be lengthy negotiations in frequencies beyond human hearing. My acoustic instruments during the third expedition recorded conversations lasting up to forty minutes between individuals whose territories never overlapped. What they find to discuss at such length I cannot imagine. The Threadbear constructs sleeping chambers from felted plant fiber and the shed filaments of its own coat, pressing the hollow hairs into a rigid lattice that provides both insulation and, apparently, a kind of acoustic resonator that amplifies territorial signals. These chambers are almost impossible to find by eye but detectable with appropriate instruments.

**Diet:** Primarily large beetles, grubs, and occasional tree fungi. The Threadbear locates grubs through the wooden substrate of fallen logs by pressing its wide muzzle flat against the bark and "listening" — whether this is acoustic or chemical detection remains under investigation.

*Field Note, Third Expedition, Day 12: The first I found was dead, having apparently fallen from the canopy. Even motionless and unfortunate it was beautiful. The coat caught the filtered light and turned it into something that looked almost liquid. I wrapped it carefully for transport and returned to find Tomás had been watching from ten paces away. He said the locals call it the "wool-ghost" and consider them unlucky to collect. I kept it, but I understood the impulse to leave it.*

## III. The Saltback Beetle *(Chrysomera halinus)*

**Habitat:** The Crystalline Margins, a coastal and sub-coastal biome characterized by salt-encrusted flats, shallow evaporative pools, and a mineral-rich substrate that crystallizes into irregular formations at the surface.

**Appearance:** The Saltback Beetle is a large scarab relative, body length up to twelve centimeters, whose elytra — the hardened wing covers — are not sclerotized cuticle in the conventional sense but are instead encrusted with mineral deposits the beetle actively harvests from its environment. As a result, no two individuals are visually identical. Young beetles are dark copper-red with smooth shells; adults accrue layers of white, orange, and occasionally violet crystal formations that make them appear, from above, like small animated geological specimens. The effect is so complete that early survey sketches misidentified several beetle specimens as rocks.

**Behavior:** The Saltback Beetle's mineral encrustation is not incidental. The crystals are arranged in specific orientations that have been shown (in work done by my colleague Dr. Ranveer Hooth, who deserves more credit than he typically receives) to reflect and refract sunlight in ways that confuse predators' spatial perception. When a colony of Saltbacks gathers on open ground — which they do regularly for communal basalt-licking — the aggregate reflection is disorienting even to human observers, producing something analogous to the shimmer over heated pavement but localized and shifting. The beetles appear aware of this effect and have been observed clustering more tightly when aerial predators approach.

**Diet:** The Saltback is a detritivore specializing in mineral-rich substrates. It digests salt minerals directly, extracting chemical energy through pathways that remain incompletely understood. Supplementary organic matter from windblown plant material.

*Field Note, First Expedition, Day 31: Sat in the middle of a congregation of perhaps four hundred Saltbacks this morning. They did not react to my presence at all. The sound of them moving was exactly like fine gravel being shuffled. The light off their shells was making my eyes water. At some point I realized I had been sitting there for two hours and had forgotten, temporarily, that I was meant to be somewhere else. A fine beetle.*

## IV. The Vein-Fox *(Canicartus venalis)*

**Habitat:** Open savanna and transitional scrub of the Veranthi interior, particularly the region known as the Painted Flats for its seasonal wildflower blooms.

**Appearance:** The Vein-Fox is a mid-sized canid, roughly the build of a large domestic dog but more lightly boned, with slender legs adapted for sustained pursuit across open ground. Its coat is its distinguishing feature: a pale tawny base coloration overlaid with a tracery of dark lines, narrow and branching, that run along the limbs, flank, and across the face in patterns that loosely resemble the branching of blood vessels — hence the common name. No two individuals share identical patterning. The ears are large and mobile, capable of rotating nearly one hundred and eighty degrees. The eyes are a remarkable gold-amber, with elongated pupils that contract to vertical slits in bright light.

**Behavior:** Vein-Foxes hunt in extended family groups of four to twelve individuals, coordinating through a combination of vocalizations and postural signals. Unlike most pack hunters, Vein-Fox groups do not have a clear dominant individual; instead, different members "lead" specific phases of a hunt according to apparent specialization — some individuals consistently initiate the pursuit while others specialize in the cutoff maneuver or the final approach. Young foxes spend approximately eighteen months in an apprenticeship phase during which they shadow experienced hunters and appear to practice specific roles. The vocalizations used during hunting are structurally complex; recordings analyzed by my colleagues suggest something approaching compositional syntax, though I advance this claim cautiously.

**Diet:** Primarily the Ribbon-Gazelle and similar savanna ungulates, supplemented by ground-nesting birds and large lizards. Vein-Fox groups have been observed returning repeatedly to kills hidden under loose soil, rationing a single large carcass across multiple days.

*Field Note, Second Expedition, Day 61: A juvenile female, perhaps eight months old, followed our supply wagon for most of the afternoon. Too young to hunt alone, too curious to stay clear. She kept exactly thirty paces back, which I began to suspect was deliberate. When we camped, she sat on a low rise and watched the cook fire until full dark. We left some dried meat at the edge of camp. It was gone by morning but she was too. I named her Proximity, which Tomás thought was excessive.*

## V. The Stonecap Urchin *(Lapidechinus cornutus)*

**Habitat:** Subterranean river systems of the Orveth foothills, specifically in the wide, slow sections where silt has accumulated and ceiling rock is close to the water surface.

**Appearance:** The Stonecap Urchin is entirely aquatic and entirely unlike the marine urchins to which its name gestures. The body is an irregular disc approximately thirty centimeters across, pale grey-white with a slightly translucent quality that allows, under good light, the suggestion of internal organs. The upper surface is covered not in spines but in blunt, rounded protrusions like inverted thimbles, hardened and calcified, which serve as the primary anchoring mechanism in fast-flowing sections. The underside bears several hundred fine cilia arranged in spiral patterns, used for both locomotion and feeding. There are five eyes positioned at the rim, each capable of independent movement, each sensitive only to movement and shadow.

**Behavior:** Stonecap Urchins are filter feeders that supplement their diet through an unusual predatory mechanism: they position themselves on the ceiling of subterranean chambers, upside down, and drop onto organisms passing below — an ambush strategy for an animal with no conventional predatory anatomy. The impact stuns smaller creatures, which the urchin then processes through the cilial feeding apparatus. They appear capable of learning the movement patterns of prey species and repositioning themselves predictively. Communities of dozens of individuals sometimes colonize a single chamber ceiling, creating a visible downward-bristling carpet of calcified knobs that researchers unfamiliar with the species have found extremely alarming.

**Diet:** Suspended organic particles (primary), supplemented by ambush predation on small blind cave fish and the larval forms of cave insects.

*Field Note, Third Expedition, Day 29: Tomás shone his lamp upward in the long chamber and I had about half a second to realize what I was looking at before the nearest one dropped. It missed. Tomás was laughing while I was still against the opposite wall. He says the locals know to look up first. I am adding this to the preparation notes for all future expeditions. Look up first.*

## VI. The Bellow-Moth *(Noctuamplius vocalus)*

**Habitat:** High-altitude meadows of the Orveth Range above the cloud-forest line, between roughly 2,800 and 3,500 meters elevation.

**Appearance:** The Bellow-Moth is a large lepidopteran, wingspan reaching twenty-two centimeters in large adults, whose wings are a remarkable velvety black on the upper surface and a complex iridescent silver-green beneath. The body is thick and heavily furred in dark grey, housing oversized thoracic musculature that serves a purpose unrelated to flight. The antennae are feathered and exceptionally long — longer than the body in adult males — and curved slightly downward, giving the resting moth an expression of weary contemplation. The proboscis is vestigial; adults do not feed.

**Behavior:** The Bellow-Moth is named for the adult male's mating call, which is produced not by any mechanism familiar in insects but by the rapid compression and release of a specialized tracheal resonance chamber in the thorax. The resulting sound is a low, reverberant pulse, felt as much as heard, audible across the open meadow at distances exceeding 800 meters. Males call continuously for the three weeks of their adult lifespan, and during peak season the high meadows fill with overlapping pulses that Tomás described, memorably, as "the sound a mountain makes when it is thinking." Females are larger and silent, selecting mates through prolonged antennal contact in a process that appears to involve chemical assessment. Larval forms spend two years underground, feeding on the roots of high-altitude sedges.

**Diet:** Adults do not feed. Larvae consume sedge root material.

*Field Note, Fourth Expedition, Day 4: First night in the high camp. The Bellow-Moths have begun their season. Lying in the tent I can feel the calls in my sternum. Three weeks in a meadow full of this sound and I understand, viscerally, why the upland Veranthi consider this month sacred. There is something in the frequency that bypasses ordinary listening. I have found myself stopping work simply to attend to it.*

## VII. The Walking Kelp *(Fucotaxis ambulans)*

**Habitat:** Shallow coastal waters and tidal zones of the Veranthi eastern coast, where subsurface rock shelving creates a complex mosaic of depth and current.

**Appearance:** The Walking Kelp is, genuinely, difficult to classify with confidence. It presents externally as a large plant — a frond structure rising up to three meters from the seafloor, brown-green, with flattened leaf-blades bearing the texture and veining of common kelp. However, it moves. The "holdfast" at the base is not rooted but is instead a muscular pad bearing dozens of short adhesive pegs that can be engaged and disengaged sequentially to produce a slow, deliberate migration across substrate. The organism also responds to stimuli — retracting blades when touched, moving away from disturbed water, and orienting toward nutrient-rich current. No nervous system has been identified. The mechanism of coordinated movement without central processing remains a significant research question.

**Behavior:** Walking Kelp migrates slowly (approximately two meters per hour under favorable conditions) following chemical gradients toward zones of higher nutrient availability. They are entirely solitary; two Walking Kelp brought into proximity will diverge without apparent conflict. Sessile phases appear to occur at night or in cold conditions, during which the organism seals its pegs against substrate and does not respond to external stimuli. Whether this is sleep, dormancy, or something without a name, I cannot say.

**Diet:** Photosynthetic and absorptive. In darkness or turbid conditions, the organism extends the edges of its blades and appears to absorb dissolved organic compounds directly from the water column.

*Field Note, Fourth Expedition, Day 19: I marked six individuals with colored dye and checked their positions each morning for two weeks. The fastest moved eleven meters in fourteen days. The slowest moved one meter and then moved back to where it had started. I have named the slow one Cautious. The fast one I have named Ambition. Both names seem, in retrospect, projections.*

## VIII. The Painted Vault Spider *(Archneila pictura)*

**Habitat:** The interior spaces of ancient Orveth cave systems, specifically in large chambers where humidity remains constant and the cave walls support crusts of mineral-rich calcite.

**Appearance:** The Painted Vault Spider is a large orb-weaver relative with a leg span of up to eighteen centimeters, body length of five centimeters. The abdomen is the immediately striking feature: a rounded, smooth surface covered in markings that appear — this is not metaphor — to have been painted. The patterns are complex, bilaterally symmetric, and employ what appears to be a limited palette: deep ochre, pale gold, black, and a blue-white that is structurally produced rather than pigmented. Different individuals in the same chamber bear markings so distinct that cave researchers have taken to naming individuals after visual resemblances (the method is not scientific but has proven durable).

**Behavior:** Painted Vault Spiders do not build conventional webs. Instead, each individual constructs a "vault" — a roughly spherical structure of silk-reinforced calcium mineral, anchored to the cave wall, large enough to contain the spider with room for prey storage. These structures take months to complete and generations of the same family may occupy and expand a single vault. The markings on the abdomen appear to function in mate selection; spectroscopic analysis has revealed that the blue-white structural coloration is produced by nano-scale structures that reflect wavelengths visible to the spider but not to humans without instrumentation, implying that what we see is only a fraction of the display.

**Diet:** Cave crickets, blind isopods, and the occasional small vertebrate. The spider subdues prey with a fast-acting venom and stores excess kills within the vault structure in a form of chemical preservation.

*Field Note, Third Expedition, Day 41: Found a vault that Tomás says has been known to the cave-guides for at least four generations. The spider inside — enormous, the diameter of my spread hand — sat perfectly still on the inner wall of her vault and watched me with all eight of her eyes for perhaps twenty minutes while I sketched. She did not appear disturbed, only attentive. I felt, with some certainty, that she was evaluating me and had reached conclusions she found unsurprising. I have rarely felt so thoroughly assessed.*

## IX. The River-Weaver Fish *(Textilopiscis fluvialis)*

**Habitat:** Fast-moving rivers in the Orveth foothills, specifically sections where submerged tree roots create a complex matrix of available anchor points.

**Appearance:** The River-Weaver Fish is an elongated freshwater fish, maximum length forty centimeters, with a flattened body adapted for life in strong current. The coloration is a variable brown-gold that matches the root systems it inhabits. Most remarkable are the pectoral fins, which are exceptionally long and finely rayed, capable of independent movement from each other. These fins serve primarily in the fish's most unusual behavior but are also used for station-keeping in current through fine adjustment rather than muscular effort.

**Behavior:** The River-Weaver constructs spawning nests by weaving plant fibers among submerged roots using its elongated pectoral fins. The construction is genuinely intricate — a basket structure of interlaced organic material, reinforced with small stones placed in specific locations to anchor the structure against current. Individual fish return to the same root system annually, rebuilding and expanding the same nest over successive seasons; the oldest known nest, marked by a research station at the Halvenmoor River Station on Veranthos's western coast, was under active maintenance for at least eleven years before the station lost funding and the monitoring lapsed. The fish "sings" during construction — a series of pulsed vibrations transmitted through the water column that appear to coordinate the activities of several males sometimes working adjacent root systems.

**Diet:** Invertebrates and small fish, hunted primarily at dusk using lateral-line detection. The River-Weaver is notably bold; individuals will approach researchers' ankles in shallow water and have been known to investigate the equipment left by the Halvenmoor River Station to a degree that occasionally required securing it against actual interference.

*Field Note, Second Expedition, Day 82: Watched one finish the southern chamber of her nest this afternoon. She placed the last stone — a white quartz pebble she had selected from what appeared to be a deliberate search of the riverbed — and then held position in the current for perhaps two minutes, fins still. Was she inspecting her work? Resting? I cannot say with confidence. But I noted it, as I note everything, against the possibility that confidence will come later.*

## X. The Dusk-Mover *(Crepusculans major)*

**Habitat:** Transitional zones between the cloud-forest and open savanna, particularly along watercourse corridors where forest cover thins but does not disappear.

**Appearance:** The Dusk-Mover is a large herbivore, roughly the mass of a draft horse but lower-slung, with a wide body supported on six legs arranged in two sets of three — a hexapodal body plan unique among Veranthi megafauna. The front pair of legs is heavier-boned and slightly longer, giving the animal a characteristic forward lean at rest. The coat is short and thick, a consistent blue-grey in adults, which in low light becomes nearly invisible against the mineral-grey soil of the transition zone. The head is wide and flat, with eyes positioned for nearly 360-degree vision and a prehensile upper lip capable of precise manipulation of browse material. Horns are absent in both sexes.

**Behavior:** Dusk-Movers are crepuscular herd animals, moving out of forest cover at dusk and returning before full daylight — a behavioral pattern so reliable that Veranthi herders use the time of their movement as a seasonal indicator. Herds of twenty to sixty individuals move in a loose column with individuals apparently taking turns in peripheral positions, cycling through exposure and shelter in a rotation that appears neither random nor individually coerced. The six-legged body plan allows an unusual gait at speed: the two rear pairs of legs move in a coordinated sequence that produces a flowing, three-phased stride with no airborne period, meaning a running Dusk-Mover produces remarkably little ground vibration relative to its mass — a significant advantage when moving through predator territory in low light.

**Diet:** Mixed browser and grazer, with seasonal preference for the long-grass of the transition zone supplemented by the leaves of low cloud-forest shrubs. The prehensile lip allows the animal to strip leaves from thorned branches without injury.

*Field Note, Fourth Expedition, Day 33: The herd crossed the river crossing at dusk, exactly as every account I had read described. I counted forty-one individuals. They moved silently, the only sound the water. The last one — a young adult trailing slightly behind — paused mid-crossing and turned its wide head directly toward me, holding my position for a long moment before continuing. I had taken great care to be downwind and still. It knew anyway. They always do. I am increasingly convinced that the business of being observed and the business of being seen are not the same thing, and that the animals of Veranthos understand the distinction better than I do.*

## Afterword

A field guide is, by nature, an act of violence against complexity. To assign names, to draw boundaries around behaviors, to reduce the irreducible to a table of attributes — these are useful lies, and I have told them here with the best intentions. The creatures of Veranthos do not require my classification to exist or to be extraordinary. They require, at present, only that we leave them enough intact landscape to continue being whatever they are.

The Interior Preservation Survey, established after my third expedition, has mapped approximately forty percent of the continental interior as ecologically sensitive. The Halvenmoor Institute has advocated for formal protection status; this effort is ongoing, underfunded, and not certain to succeed.

A fifth expedition is planned. There are more creatures in this continent than I have pages. I intend to find them.

*— E. Voss*
