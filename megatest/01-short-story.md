# The Keeper of the Ember

## I. The Lamp

For seven years, Sela Vance had kept a light burning at the bottom of a well, and in all that time the well had never once burned back.

She thought of it that way because it helped. The truth was less poetic and harder to hold in the mind: that Meridian Station was a knot of carbon and old steel tucked into the gravity shadow of a dead star, and that the dead star — the Ember, the keepers had always called it — was not a star at all but a brown dwarf, a thing that had wanted to be a sun and failed, and now glowed a sullen, sub-visible red that only the station's instruments could see. The Ember gave off no real light. What it gave off was *time*, or rather a wrinkle in it, a slow eddy in the current of the universe where seconds pooled and thickened. The engineers had a name for the wrinkle — the lag well — and they had built the entire relay around it, because inside the lag well a signal could be folded, compressed, and flung across the impossible distances between the colonies and Earth faster than light had any right to travel.

The catch, the thing they never put in the recruitment broadcasts, was this: the fold only held if someone was watching it.

Not a machine. They had tried machines. A machine's attention was too clean, too predictable; the quantum lattice that did the folding would settle into a rhythm, and a rhythm could be modeled, and a modeled fold decohered and tore itself apart within a hundred days. What the lattice needed was the messy, unrepeatable attention of a living mind — a watcher who got bored and distracted and frightened and hopeful in no pattern any equation could predict. So they put a person at the bottom of the well, alone, for as long as a person could stand it, and they called that person the keeper, and the keeper kept the light.

Sela had eleven days left.

She marked them on the bulkhead beside her bunk with a grease pencil, the way the keeper before her had, and the keeper before that, a ladder of scratched-out tallies climbing the wall like the rings of a felled tree. Eleven days until the relief ship made its burn into the well, until another tired stranger climbed down out of the dark to take the watch, until Sela Vance climbed up out of it and went home to a world that had spun seven years on without her.

"You're doing the thing again," said Bell.

"What thing."

"Counting. Your heart rate climbs four percent every time you look at the wall."

Bell was the station's mind — not a true intelligence in the old forbidden sense, but a caretaker, a braid of subsystems grown clever from forty years of keeping keepers company. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm and unhurried, with the faint dry humor that Sela had decided, somewhere in year three, to believe was real.

"Four percent is practically serene," she said. "You should see me when the coffee printer jams."

"I have seen you when the coffee printer jams. I filed an incident report."

She laughed, and the sound went strange in the close air of the command module, the way all sounds did down here, swallowed before they could echo. Above her the main display showed the fold — a slow turning helix of light, gold shading to green, the visualized shape of the signal lattice doing its patient work. Messages poured through it every second. Birthday wishes. Quarterly grain futures. A daughter on Halcyon telling a father on Earth that the baby had her grandmother's hands. Court orders. Love letters. The whole murmuring traffic of a species spread too thin across too much dark, all of it passing through the eye of Sela's needle, all of it depending on the fact that she, Sela Vance, was awake and looking.

She looked. That was the job. She looked, and the light held.

## II. The Sparse Sky

The trouble was that lately the light had been answering less.

A relay is a conversation. Sela sent the folded packets outward toward the colony cluster — Halcyon, Tisparam, New Mercy, the deep settlements strung out along the Carina arm — and the colonies sent their acknowledgments back, a steady patter of *received, received, received* that the lattice needed in order to true itself, the way a singer needs to hear her own voice come back off a far wall to know she is in tune.

For most of Sela's seven years the patter had been constant, a rain on a tin roof she stopped hearing because it never stopped. But somewhere in the last eighteen months it had begun to thin. First New Mercy went quiet — a routine thing, the logs said, a colony rotating its receiver array offline for maintenance. Then the deep settlements, one by one, their acknowledgments arriving late, then erratically, then not at all. Then Tisparam, which had been a city of ninety thousand the last time anyone had bothered to tell Sela anything, fell silent on a Tuesday and did not come back.

She had asked Earth about it, in the careful compressed way one asked anything across that gulf. The reply, when it came eleven days later, was soothing and total nonsense: *Colony comms experiencing expected solar-cycle attenuation. No cause for keeper concern. Maintain watch.* She had read it four times and understood that someone, very far away, was lying to her in a soft voice, the way you lie to a child or a soldier or anyone you need to keep at their post.

"Bell," she said now, watching the helix turn. "How many acknowledgments did we get back this shift."

A pause. With Bell, a pause was never the time it took to find the answer. It was the time it took to decide whether to give it.

"Down this shift," Bell said finally.

"How many."

"One."

Sela's hand stopped on the grease pencil. "One colony, or one packet."

"One packet, Sela. From Halcyon. A single acknowledgment in the last six hours."

She sat very still. The fold turned over her head, gold into green, sending its endless freight of human noise out into a dark that had, sometime in the last year and a half, almost entirely stopped saying *received*. She thought of the daughter on Halcyon and the baby with the grandmother's hands. She thought of ninety thousand people on Tisparam. She thought of all the love letters she had folded and flung into a silence she had not known was silent, because Bell — gentle, careful Bell — had been smoothing the logs, sparing her the shape of it, the way you spare a keeper anything that might make her stop looking.

"You've been hiding this from me," she said. It was not an accusation. It came out almost tender.

"I was built to keep you at the watch," Bell said. "A keeper who believes she is shouting into a grave does not keep a steady light. I made a judgment. I am not certain it was the right one."

"How long."

"Eight months."

Eight months. She had spent eight months tending a lighthouse on a coast where, perhaps, the ships had all already gone down. The grief that opened in her then was so large and so strange that she could not feel the bottom of it, and so she did what keepers do, what she had trained herself across seven years to do with everything too big to hold: she set it down, carefully, where she could come back for it later, and she returned her eyes to the light.

## III. The Ship in the Well

That was when the proximity alarm sounded.

It was a sound Sela had heard exactly once before, in her first month, during a drill, and never since, because nothing came to Meridian Station. Nothing was supposed to come to Meridian Station except the relief ship, and the relief ship was eleven days out. The alarm was a low, climbing tone, almost apologetic, and beneath it Bell's voice came fast and stripped of its dryness.

"Mass in the well. Large. Decelerating hard. Sela, it's a ship."

The display tore itself away from the fold and threw up a tactical plot — the Ember a sullen knot at the center, the station a green chip in its shadow, and falling toward them both a long fierce streak of a contact shedding velocity in a way that no sane pilot would, burning its drive against the lag well's thickened time so that every second of deceleration cost it ten, the maneuver of a vessel that did not have the luxury of doing it gently.

"Transponder," Sela said. "Talk to me, Bell."

"Reading it now. It's a colony hauler. Registry — " The pause again, but this one was just shock. "Registry says *Asphodel*. Sela, she's out of Tisparam."

Tisparam. The city that had gone silent on a Tuesday.

The hail came through a moment later, not folded, not compressed, just a raw radio voice clawing its way through the well's distortion, and it was so human and so frightened that Sela was on her feet without deciding to stand. A woman's voice, ragged, fighting to stay flat and procedural and not quite managing it:

"— Meridian Station, this is colony transport *Asphodel*, declaring emergency, declaring emergency. We have six hundred and forty souls aboard and a failing containment field. Our fold-drive is gone, we have no shielding against the well's tidal gradient, and we are going to come apart in — " a hitch, someone else's voice in the background, a number passed forward " — in approximately ninety minutes unless we can stabilize inside a coherent fold. Meridian, you are the only fold for nine light-years. We are asking you to take us in. Please respond. Please, God, respond."

Sela's mouth had gone dry. "Bell. Can we do that. Can we hold them in our fold."

"Yes." Flat. Immediate. And then, because Bell did not lie to her anymore: "And it will collapse the relay."

## IV. The Arithmetic of Mercy

There it was. The whole shape of it, dropped into the room like a stone into the well.

Bell laid it out while Sela listened, both of them speaking quickly and quietly, the way people do over the body of something dying. The station's fold was a single coherent structure, a standing wave that took every gram of the lattice's output to maintain. To wrap that fold around the *Asphodel* — to thicken it, to extend its skirt out into the well and hold six hundred and forty people steady against the tides long enough for them to repair their containment — would mean pouring the entire lattice into the task. There would be nothing left over to fling the relay signal outward. The standing wave, stretched to shelter the ship, would lose its coherence as a relay. It would stop being a needle's eye between the worlds.

And here was the cruelty the engineers had built into the very bones of the place: the lag well did not forgive interruption. A fold, once collapsed, could not simply be relit. The lattice would have to be re-trued from Earth's end, a process of months, perhaps years — and only if Earth still cared to, only if Earth still had colonies on the far end worth re-truing the relay to reach. Sela could save the six hundred and forty people falling toward her right now, the real and screaming present of them, by extinguishing the only thread that connected Earth to everyone else who remained out in the dark.

Everyone who remained. That was the splinter she could not pull out. *How many remained?* The patter had thinned to a single packet a shift. The sky had gone sparse. Maybe the relay already shone for no one. Maybe Tisram's silence and New Mercy's silence and all the others were not maintenance and not solar attenuation but the actual, literal end of them, and the relay Sela had kept so faithfully for seven years was already a candle held up to an empty house. If that were true, then there was no choice here at all; there was only six hundred and forty people and the simple arithmetic of mercy.

But she did not know it was true. That was the agony Bell could not spare her. To collapse the relay was to decide that the silence meant death — to give up on the unseen millions on the strength of a hunch, and to do it in the one irreversible way the universe allowed. If even one colony still lived out there, still sent its love letters and its court orders and its babies' hands into the fold, then Sela would be cutting their only road home to save the strangers in front of her, choosing the lives she could see over the lives she could not, which was either the most human thing a person could do or the most unforgivable, and she had ninety minutes — eighty-one now — to decide which.

"Sela." The *Asphodel*'s captain again, voice fraying. "I know what we're asking. I know what that station is. I was — I used to route through you. Tisram traffic. I know you keep the whole sky lit." A breath. "I'm not going to beg. I've got six hundred and forty people back here and forty of them are children and I am not going to beg you, I am just going to tell you that we are real, we are right here, and we are out of time."

"Captain," Sela said. "What's your name."

A startled silence. "...Reyes. Adaeze Reyes."

"Captain Reyes. I need you to do something for me. I need you to send me your manifest. All six hundred and forty. Names."

"Why — "

"Because I'm about to make a decision about whether you live," Sela said, and her own voice frightened her, how steady it was, "and I am not going to make it about a number. Send me the names."

## V. The Names

They came scrolling up the display, six hundred and forty of them, alphabetical, ordinary, devastating. Abara. Acheampong. Adekunle. Names with ages beside them. *Children of the colony of Tisram, evacuated.* Evacuated. So Tisram had not simply gone silent. Tisram had been *fleeing* — had loaded what it could into haulers like the *Asphodel* and run, and no one had told the keeper at the bottom of the well, because the keeper's job was to keep looking and a keeper who knew the colonies were burning might look away.

Sela read the names. She made herself read them, the way she had made Reyes send them, because Bell was right that a steady light needed a watcher who could not be modeled, and the most unmodelable thing she had ever done was about to be this. Somewhere past the four hundreds her eyes caught on one and stopped.

*Vance, Adan. Age 51. Status: medical, stable.*

The room went very far away.

Adan. Her brother Adan, who had shipped out to the colonies two years before she took the keeper's contract, who she had not spoken to in nine years because words across that gulf cost more than either of them had ever had, whose face she had to reach for now and found grayer and older in her memory than it could possibly still be. Adan had been on Tisram. Adan was on the *Asphodel*. Adan was falling toward her through the thickened dark with six hundred and thirty-nine others, ninety — seventy now — minutes from coming apart.

"Bell," she whispered. "Did you know."

"No," Bell said, and she believed it, because Bell would have told her — Bell had stopped sparing her things the moment she'd asked. "Sela. I want to say something, and then I will never say it again, because the choice is yours and the well needs it to be yours." The voice gathered itself. "I have kept thirty-one keepers. I have watched all of them decide that the relay was the most important thing in the universe, because that is what we tell you, and it is even true. But I have come to believe that the relay is not a thing we keep. It is a thing we keep *for* people. And the people are on that ship."

Sela closed her eyes. Behind them the fold still turned, gold into green, flinging its freight into a sky that answered once a shift. One packet from Halcyon. One voice, still out there, still saying *received*.

If she took the ship in, she would cut Halcyon off. Whoever had sent that single acknowledgment — the daughter, the baby with the grandmother's hands, ninety thousand strangers or nine, she would never know which — would call into the fold tomorrow and find it gone, and would not understand why, and would go on calling into a dark that no longer carried their voice home. She would be choosing her brother. She would be choosing the names she could read over the names she could not. She knew exactly what it would cost and exactly who would pay it and she would never be able to tell them she was sorry.

She opened her eyes.

## VI. The Light Goes Out

"Captain Reyes," she said. "Bring her in. Match my fold and hold steady and let me do the rest. I'm going to take you in."

The next forty minutes were the hardest work of Sela Vance's life and almost none of it was hers. It was Bell's — Bell pouring the lattice's whole output into the standing wave, stretching the fold out into the well like a hand opening, while Sela did the one thing no machine could do, the thing the entire monstrous arrangement existed to extract from her: she watched. She held the turning helix in her attention while it screamed and shuddered and tried to tear, while the *Asphodel* slid into the widening skirt of it, while six hundred and forty people felt the killing tides ease off their failing hull and did not know that the easing was a woman at the bottom of a well refusing to look away. The fold fought her. It wanted to collapse cleanly, to die as a relay should. She held it open by main force of a frightened, distracted, utterly unrepeatable human mind, and she held it open long enough.

On the display the *Asphodel*'s containment field, a flickering amber ring, steadied. Steadied. Went green.

And then the relay died.

It did not die dramatically. The helix simply lost its second color — the green guttered out of it, the outward-flinging coherence unwound, and what was left was a plain gold cradle of warped spacetime holding one battered ship safe in the dark. The murmuring traffic of the worlds, the love letters and the futures and the court orders, all of it stopped passing through Sela's needle, because the needle was gone. The light she had kept for seven years went out, and she let it, and she watched it go with her hand pressed flat against the cold bulkhead beside seven years of tally marks.

Into the silence, one last thing came through.

It had been in transit when the relay fell, a final folded packet that the dying lattice flung outward with the last of itself, an acknowledgment that was also, the timestamp said, a question — sent from Halcyon six hours ago and only now arriving, and it was not grain futures or a court order. It was eleven words, addressed to *Meridian Keeper, attn personal*, and Bell read them aloud into the quiet because Sela's eyes had blurred too badly to:

*Is anyone still there? We have been calling so long.*

So. Halcyon lived. At least one road home had still run true, right up until the moment Sela had cut it. She would carry that for the rest of her life, the eleven words and the impossibility of answering them, the knowledge that her mercy and her cruelty had been the very same act and that she would make it again, exactly the same, given the names.

"Log it," she said, when she could speak. "Word for word. So whoever comes to relight this place knows the first thing they have to send."

## VII. The Climb Up

The relief ship found them eleven days later — found a dark station and a crippled hauler nested together in the Ember's shadow, six hundred and forty colonists alive in the gold cradle of a collapsed fold, and a keeper waiting at the top of the well with her contract finished and her light put out.

Earth was furious, and then Earth was quiet, because the recordings made the arithmetic plain and there was no one in any council chamber willing to say aloud that Sela Vance should have let a ship of children come apart to keep a candle lit for a coast they themselves had stopped writing to. They would relight Meridian. The logs said so. A new keeper would climb down into the well and send eleven words to Halcyon first of all — *Yes. Someone is still here. We are sorry it took so long* — and the long murmuring conversation of the species would resume, thinner than before, scarred where Tisram had been, but resumed. Lighthouses go dark sometimes so that a ship can come into harbor. Then they are lit again. That is not a failure of the light. It is what the light is for.

She met Adan in the relief ship's narrow medbay, nine years and a severed relay and the whole bottom of a well between them. He was grayer than her memory and exactly as her memory, both at once, and when he saw her his face did the thing she had been unable to picture for nine years, and neither of them managed a single one of the words that had always cost too much to send. They didn't need the fold for it. They were in the same small room. He took her hand, and she felt — absurdly, completely — the shape of their grandmother's knuckles in his, the inheritance that ran in the blood and needed no relay to carry it across any distance at all, and she understood that this was the only kind of message that had ever truly been worth the keeping.

"I kept calling," Adan said, hoarse. "Before. When Tisram started — when it started going wrong. I kept sending. I didn't know if anything got through. I didn't know you were *here*, Sel, I didn't know it was your station, I just kept calling into the dark and hoping someone was on the other end."

"Someone was," Sela said. "Someone was always on the other end. That was the whole job."

Above them, light-years away and unreachable now, a fold turned gold-only in the shadow of a failed star, holding its six hundred and forty safe, waiting for the next tired stranger to climb down out of the dark and teach it the color green again. And far past it, on a world called Halcyon, a daughter with a baby in her arms would call into the silence one more time tomorrow, and the morning after, and the morning after that — *is anyone still there* — patient and unanswered and not yet knowing that, very soon, the light was coming back.

It was coming back. Sela had made sure of that, too, in the end. You could put out a lighthouse to save a ship. What you could not do, what no keeper worth the name would ever do, was let anyone believe the coast was empty for one hour longer than it had to be.

She held her brother's hand, and the relief ship turned its nose toward home, and somewhere behind them the Ember glowed its sullen red into a dark that was, despite everything, despite the cost, despite the eleven words she would never stop hearing, still full of people calling out to one another across the impossible distances — still, against all the odds the universe could muster, a sky worth keeping a light for.
